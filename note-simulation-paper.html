Why simulation exercises still run on paper

Paper is not the central problem. Fragmented coordination, incomplete observation, and learning that disappears after the exercise are.

Ahmed Sorour · July 2026 · 5 minute read

Many simulation exercises are held together by familiar materials: printed injects, facilitator folders, spreadsheets, messaging groups, radios, and handwritten observation forms. These tools are flexible. Teams know how to use them. They also work when connectivity does not.

So the problem is not that paper exists. The problem is that the information needed to run and learn from the exercise is distributed across people, files, channels, and moments in time.

The exercise has several realities at once

Controllers need to know what should happen next. Facilitators need to respond to the players in front of them. Role players need timely instructions. Evaluators need to observe decisions against objectives. Leadership needs to understand whether the exercise is still testing the intended capability.

Each group sees only part of the exercise. When coordination depends on informal updates, the exercise can continue while its shared picture quietly breaks apart.

Good improvisation can hide weak infrastructure

Experienced exercise teams are very good at compensating. They rewrite an inject, call a role player, move a scenario forward, or reconstruct the timeline after the session. That skill is valuable. It can also make a fragile process appear reliable.

The cost becomes visible later: observations without precise timing, decisions without context, duplicated notes, actions without owners, and an after-action review built from partial memory.

An exercise should test the participants, not the administrative endurance of the exercise team.

What a useful digital layer should change

Digitisation is worthwhile only when it improves the work around the exercise. A useful system should:

  • give the control team a shared, current view of the scenario;
  • preserve the link between objectives, injects, expected actions, and observations;
  • record changes without preventing facilitators from adapting;
  • support different communication channels without losing the common timeline;
  • make evidence easier to review during debriefing;
  • carry lessons and actions into the next exercise;
  • continue to function when connectivity is limited or unavailable.

It should not force every exercise into one format. A tabletop exercise, drill, functional exercise, and full-scale exercise create different coordination demands. The system should provide structure without pretending that the exercise is predictable.

Where AI may help — and where it should stop

AI can help draft scenario material, check consistency, suggest injects, organise observations, and identify patterns for review. Those are assistance tasks. Exercise directors still decide what is appropriate, evaluators still interpret performance, and participants still own the decisions being tested.

The boundary should be visible. A suggestion must not quietly become an exercise fact or an evaluation judgement.

The real design question

The question is not whether paper should disappear. It is whether everyone who needs to coordinate, observe, and learn can work from a reliable shared structure — during the exercise and after it.

That is the problem behind SimReady and the wider exercise work I am developing. The goal is not a more technological exercise. It is a more coherent one.
