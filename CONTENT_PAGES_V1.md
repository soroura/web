## Home

### Hero

Health teams are asked to prepare for extraordinary situations with fragmented tools: courses that end at certification, exercises coordinated on paper, assessments trapped in spreadsheets, and lessons that are difficult to carry into the next response.

My work starts inside that gap — working with practitioners to turn recurring field problems into useful tools, methods, and shared infrastructure. The projects connect learning, simulation, assessment, evidence, and improvement while remaining grounded in the realities of public-health and emergency settings.

### The connected cycle

- **Learn:** Turn guidance into usable learning, practice, and support for trainers and teams.
- **Prepare:** Translate plans and standards into roles, workflows, scenarios, and decisions.
- **Exercise:** Give teams realistic ways to test coordination before the real event.
- **Assess:** Capture readiness, performance, and gaps in a form people can act on.
- **Improve:** Carry lessons into accountable actions, better tools, and the next cycle.

### How I work

- **Field-led:** Start with a workflow that is failing or taking too much effort.
- **Context-aware:** Treat language, connectivity, staffing, and policy as design requirements.
- **Human-accountable:** AI can organise, compare, draft, and suggest. People decide.
- **Measurable:** A useful tool should make capability and improvement easier to see.

### Collaboration

I am looking for practitioners with real operational problems, teams able to test a defined tool, researchers who can help evaluate it, and technical collaborators who understand public systems and constrained settings.

## Builds

### Introduction

These are connected experiments in one programme of work: helping health teams learn, prepare, exercise, assess, and improve. Some are active platforms; others are research or programme infrastructure. Each begins with a problem observed in practice.

### Flagship work

#### SimReady

Simulation exercises are among the best ways to test preparedness, yet much of the work is still coordinated through documents, spreadsheets, messaging groups, and memory. SimReady explores how one platform can support the exercise lifecycle from design and conduct to evaluation and learning.

**Current stage:** In active development.  
**Collaboration sought:** Exercise practitioners, pilot teams, evaluators, and specialists in offline-first and multilingual systems.

#### Assessment and improvement tools

Assessments often produce a score and a report, then lose momentum. This work explores how WHO-aligned readiness, safety, emergency-care, and resilience instruments can move from data collection into prioritised actions and visible follow-up.

**Current stage:** Prototyping and programme development.  
**Collaboration sought:** Hospital teams, quality and preparedness leads, assessment specialists, and implementation researchers.

#### Emergency-care learning infrastructure

Training completion is not the same as capability. This work examines how emergency-care programmes can better support trainers, local delivery, practice, evidence, and continued improvement after a course ends.

**Current stage:** Programme work and product exploration.  
**Collaboration sought:** Trainers, programme leads, learners, curriculum specialists, and teams evaluating changes in practice.

#### TraumaApp emergency-department analysis

Traditional registries rarely capture the full sequence and timing of emergency-department care. This MSc research uses real-world TraumaApp data to examine factors associated with length of stay and time to definitive care.

**Current stage:** Research in progress.  
**Collaboration sought:** Emergency-care researchers and practitioners interested in operational data, time-to-care measures, and service improvement.

## About

### Opening

From frontline decisions to the systems, learning, evidence, and tools that help people prepare.

### The synthesis

Offshore medicine taught me what it means to make decisions with limited support. Emergency departments taught me how quickly individual expertise reaches the limits of the system around it. Public-health and WHO work showed me the distance between a sound framework and a team being able to use it under pressure.

Building became a way to work on that distance. Not technology for its own sake, but practical infrastructure that helps teams learn, prepare, exercise, assess, and improve.

### What I am trying to build now

I am developing a family of focused tools, methods, and knowledge resources rather than one all-purpose platform. They share a set of constraints: they must be useful to practitioners, accountable to human judgement, realistic about institutional workflows, and capable of working in Arabic, offline, or with limited resources where necessary.

### First note summaries

#### From fragmented tools to a preparedness cycle

Why courses, plans, exercises, assessments, and improvement work should not be treated as separate activities — and what becomes possible when learning is carried from one stage to the next.

#### Why simulation exercises still run on paper

Paper is not the problem by itself. The deeper problem is fragmented coordination, weak information flow, and lessons that are hard to preserve for the next exercise.

#### AI should suggest; humans should decide

A working principle for applied AI in public systems: make assistance visible, make assumptions inspectable, and keep responsibility with the people doing the work.

## Collaborate

### Opening

### Ways to collaborate

- Bring a field workflow, programme, assessment, or exercise that needs a better tool.
- Offer a team or setting for a bounded, well-documented pilot.
- Review methods, scenarios, indicators, or operational assumptions.
- Contribute expertise in offline systems, multilingual design, interoperability, data governance, or responsible AI.
- Help design research that tests usability, learning, preparedness, adoption, or service outcomes.
- Connect a useful component to an institution capable of maintaining it.
