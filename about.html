About

Most of what I do sits in one gap: between training people and seeing practice change.

We train health workers well. Then the course ends, and whether anything changes in practice becomes hard to see. That gap is where my work at WHO Egypt sits, and it is what the tools I build are trying to close.

What building has taught me so far

I started building digital tools to learn what AI can change, first in the way I work and then, if it holds up, in health care more widely. I keep a register of every build, the ones that worked and the ones that did not. A few lessons keep coming back.

The hard questions are not technical

Being inside real hospital and primary care assessments taught me which questions decide whether a tool gets used. Does the assessor have the time? An assessment is a day against a schedule, so ten extra minutes per criterion is fatal. Does the tool fit what the facility already does, or does it become a second process running alongside the first? Evidence comes as documents, interviews and observation, each with its own rhythm. A tool that handles only one leaves the assessor holding a paper notebook.

When you don't know who a tool is for, build it both ways

For patient safety assessment I could not tell whether the right shape was a visiting peer team or a self-assessment the organisation runs itself. So I built it both ways to find out: one platform centred on the assessor in the field, one centred on the organisation running its own cycle. The pilots are how I am finding out.

"Does it work?" is the easy test

The harder one: does it save the user time, does it fit the day they already have, and if it needs more people involved, is that essential, or a cost I am passing to them and calling a feature? Held to that test, the tools I am proudest of technically are often the ones I can least defend. AI can organise, compare, draft and suggest. People decide.

Where I have been

Health systems (2018 — present)

I have worked with WHO Egypt since 2018: auditing facilities for the 100 Million Healthy Lives campaign, then in the Minister of Health's technical office, then as a medical officer at the UN COVID-19 isolation facility. Since 2022 I have been Emergency Officer, Health Systems.

I coordinate Egypt's national rollout of WHO's Basic Emergency Care programme with the African Federation for Emergency Medicine, and I have been working on the national mass casualty management programme and on hospital resilience. The work has widened into hospital quality, primary care, and migrant and refugee health, including a project with JICA and the Ministry of Health and Population that I coordinated across assessment, capacity building and procurement.

What this work keeps showing me is the distance between a sound framework and a team being able to use it on an ordinary Tuesday, let alone under pressure.

Alongside WHO, I work with Bupa Global, a private health insurer, where I decide which treatments are funded, weighing the treating doctor's plan against the policy and the clinical evidence.

Learning and building (2023 — present)

I went back to study: a master's in public health, in health services management, from the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine, and a master's in data science for health and social care from the University of Edinburgh. Then I started building, with AI as a development partner. The first three tools are on the Builds page.

Where it started (2005 — 2018)

I qualified in medicine at Ain Shams University in 2005 and spent seven years as an offshore doctor on drilling rigs, where the nearest hospital was a helicopter flight away and the medevac decision was mine. I also trained in emergency medicine at Alexandria University Hospitals. Those years left two convictions: systems matter more than individual heroics, and the tools people use under pressure are rarely designed by people who have used them.

What I believe

Technology risk in health care is mostly not technical.

A tool that works can still fail: because it costs the user time, because it duplicates a process the facility already runs, or because nobody was waiting for it. The model being accurate is necessary and nowhere near enough.

AI should suggest. Humans should decide. A tool's job is to make that boundary visible, and to give people the structure they need to use their own judgement well.

And this can be built from Cairo. The problems are close, and the solutions have to work offline, in Arabic, with unreliable connectivity. That constraint is a design advantage.
