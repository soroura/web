# Background 1 — Website Content Baseline and Collaboration Brief

**Working document — 11 July 2026**

## Purpose

This document records the current state of soroura.org and provides the first content brief for its next version. The aim is not to turn the site into a conventional portfolio or a list of credentials. It is to make the larger idea behind the work visible, show credible evidence that the work is already underway, and give the right people a clear reason to collaborate.

No claims marked **Confirm before publishing** should be copied into the website until Ahmed verifies their current status, ownership, public name, and disclosure boundaries.

## 1. What the current website says

The current site presents Ahmed Sorour as a public health and disaster medicine specialist who works at the intersection of emergency care, preparedness, health systems, data science, and applied technology.

Its central argument is:

> The people doing the hardest work in emergencies often have inadequate tools. Ahmed has seen that gap from the frontline, from health-system implementation, and from policy work — so he started building.

The current story is organised around three career arcs:

1. **The frontline:** offshore and emergency medicine, clinical decisions under pressure, and direct experience of systems under constraint.
2. **The systems:** emergency-care implementation, disaster preparedness, training, assessment, and national or regional coordination.
3. **The building:** translating recurring operational problems into practical platforms and tools.

The site currently showcases three principal builds:

- **SimReady:** an AI-augmented platform for planning, designing, conducting, and evaluating simulation exercises.
- **National Assessment Platform:** a framework for consolidating health-facility assessment and improvement tools.
- **TraumaApp dissertation analysis:** applied data-science research into emergency-department length of stay and time to definitive care.

It also presents Basic Emergency Care scale-up, mass-casualty exercises, hospital emergency operations exercises, regional training, and trauma-system work as field engagements.

## 2. What is already working

- The identity is distinctive: not an academic, consultant, or technology founder, but a practitioner who builds.
- The voice is direct, grounded, reflective, and specific.
- The frontline-to-systems-to-building arc gives the work credibility and coherence.
- The site avoids inflated startup language and lets operational experience carry the argument.
- The visual system is restrained and appropriate: an editorial “field notes” aesthetic with a single ember-red accent.
- The existing phrase “the tools haven’t caught up with the people” is a strong organising idea.

## 3. What the current website does not yet explain

The site shows individual projects, but it does not yet reveal the system connecting them.

Visitors can see that Ahmed builds tools, but they cannot easily answer:

- What larger future are these tools intended to create?
- Which parts of that future already exist?
- Which projects are experiments, which are active products, and which are programme work?
- Who are the intended users and beneficiaries?
- What kinds of collaboration would be useful now?
- What could a clinician, public-health practitioner, researcher, designer, engineer, institution, or funder contribute?

The result is credible but somewhat closed. It reads as a record of work already done, rather than an open invitation to help shape what comes next.

These projects are not random applications. Together, they suggest one programme of work:

> Build practical infrastructure that helps health teams learn, prepare, assess, decide, and improve — under the real constraints of public-health and emergency settings.

That infrastructure has several recurring characteristics:

- starts from a real operational problem;
- supports practitioners rather than replacing their judgement;
- connects guidance to action;

- uses AI as a structured assistant, with humans retaining responsibility for decisions.

## 5. The idea to showcase

### Recommended positioning

**Better preparedness needs better connections between learning, practice, evidence, and improvement.**

The work connects emergency-care learning, simulation, readiness assessment, operational decision support, and improvement. Each project starts with a recurring field problem: training that is difficult to scale, exercises managed through paper and messaging apps, assessments that stop at spreadsheets, or evidence that cannot be turned into timely action.

The ambition is not one all-purpose platform. It is an interoperable family of useful tools, methods, and knowledge resources that help teams move through a common cycle:

**Learn → Prepare → Exercise → Assess → Improve**

### Short homepage version

Health teams are asked to prepare for extraordinary situations with fragmented tools: courses that end at certification, exercises coordinated on paper, assessments trapped in spreadsheets, and lessons that are difficult to carry into the next response.

My work starts inside that gap — working with practitioners to turn recurring field problems into useful tools, methods, and shared infrastructure. The projects connect learning, simulation, assessment, evidence, and improvement while remaining grounded in the realities of public-health and emergency settings.

### One-sentence version

Practical, field-ready systems can connect how health teams learn, prepare, exercise, assess, and improve.

## 6. Collaboration briefing

### Working title

**Building the missing infrastructure between health guidance and field practice**

### The problem

Health organisations already have standards, manuals, courses, assessment tools, and experienced people. The failure is often in the connections between them.

Training may not connect to performance. Exercises may not connect to improvement plans. Assessments may not connect to follow-up. Evidence may not reach the people who need to act. Valuable institutional knowledge remains distributed across documents, inboxes, spreadsheets, and individuals.

### The proposition

Create a practical layer between guidance and field practice: small, interoperable tools and methods that help teams learn, prepare, exercise, assess, and improve.

This work should be:

- **field-led:** shaped with the people who will use it;
- **open to scrutiny:** methods, assumptions, and AI suggestions must be inspectable;
- **human-accountable:** AI can organise, draft, compare, or suggest; people decide;
- **context-aware:** language, connectivity, staffing, policy, and workflow constraints are part of the design;
- **measurable:** every tool should make it easier to see whether capability or performance improved;
- **modular:** teams should be able to adopt one useful component without buying into an entire ecosystem.

### What already exists

The portfolio includes working or developing concepts across:

- simulation-exercise design, conduct, and evaluation;
- emergency-care learning and trainer support;
- health-facility and hospital-readiness assessment;
- mass-casualty and EMT preparedness;
- workforce planning;
- evidence scanning and structured decision support;
- emergency-department data analysis;
- reusable design and knowledge infrastructure.

**Confirm before publishing:** the public names, maturity, ownership, deployment evidence, release counts, user counts, geographic coverage, partnerships, and links for every item.

### Who this is for

- emergency-care and disaster-medicine practitioners;
- ministries of health and public-health agencies;
- hospital leaders, quality teams, and preparedness teams;
- simulation designers, facilitators, and evaluators;
- trainers and learning-programme leads;
- EMT and mass-casualty specialists;
- health-service and implementation researchers;
- product designers, software engineers, data scientists, and AI practitioners interested in field constraints;
- institutions able to pilot, evaluate, fund, or responsibly scale useful components.

### What collaboration could look like

1. **Field partnership** — bring a real workflow, programme, assessment, or exercise that needs a better tool.
2. **Pilot site** — test a defined component in a real setting and document what works, fails, and changes.
3. **Subject-matter contribution** — review methods, scenarios, indicators, clinical or operational assumptions, and evaluation criteria.
4. **Technical collaboration** — contribute to offline-first delivery, multilingual interfaces, interoperability, data governance, or responsible AI.
5. **Research and evaluation** — help design studies that test usability, adoption, learning, preparedness, and system outcomes.
6. **Institutional partnership** — align a tool with an existing programme and create a realistic path to maintenance and scale.
7. **Documentation and translation** — improve clarity, localisation, implementation guidance, and reusable knowledge resources.

### The invitation

> If you are working on emergency care, preparedness, health-workforce capability, simulation, assessment, or responsible AI for public systems, I would like to compare notes. Bring a real problem, a setting where it matters, or expertise that can make the work more rigorous. The aim is not to add technology to an existing process. It is to build something practitioners will still choose to use when conditions are difficult.

## 7. Recommended content architecture

### Home — explain the idea

The homepage should answer five questions in order:

1. **What problem do I work on?** The missing infrastructure between health guidance and field practice.
2. **What am I building toward?** A connected learn–prepare–exercise–assess–improve cycle.
3. **What evidence is there?** A small set of flagship projects and real programme work.
4. **Why am I positioned to do this?** Frontline, system, research, and building experience.
5. **How can someone participate?** Specific collaboration routes, not a generic “say hello.”

Recommended sections:

- Hero: the core problem and one-sentence proposition
- The operating cycle: Learn / Prepare / Exercise / Assess / Improve
- Flagship work: 3–5 selected projects
- From the field: current notes, findings, and lessons
- How I work: field-led, human-accountable, context-aware, measurable
- Collaboration invitation: concrete partner types and needs
- Brief about section and contact

### Builds — show a portfolio, not a list

Group projects by the problem they solve rather than only by product name:

- Learning and capability
- Exercises and readiness
- Assessment and improvement
- Evidence and decisions
- Shared infrastructure

Each project entry should include:

- the field problem;
- who it is for;
- what exists now;
- evidence or maturity;
- what is being tested next;
- the specific collaboration sought;
- public link, demonstration, or note when available.

### About — establish the right to work on the problem

Keep the three arcs, but shorten the chronology. Give more space to the synthesis: how clinical isolation, emergency departments, national implementation, facilitation, research, and building changed Ahmed's understanding of the problem.

End with a forward-looking section: **What I am trying to build now.**

### Field Notes — demonstrate thinking in public

Separate evidence of activity from actual field notes.

Recommended categories:

- Field observations
- Build notes
- Methods and tools
- Research notes
- Talks, training, and publications

Publish a small number of substantive notes before expanding the index. Strong first topics:

- Why exercises still run on paper
- What an assessment must do after the score is calculated
- AI should suggest; humans should decide
- Offline-first is an operational requirement, not a technical preference
- What training completion fails to tell us
- From fragmented tools to a preparedness cycle

### Contact / Collaborate — qualify the invitation

Rename the purpose of the page from “Say Hello” to **Collaborate** while keeping the tone welcoming.

Include:

- the problems Ahmed wants to discuss;
- the types of collaborators sought;
- examples of useful first messages;
- disclosure that formal WHO work must follow the appropriate institutional channel;
- LinkedIn as the only published direct-contact route, with formal institutional work continuing through the appropriate organisational process.

## 8. Proposed phased update plan

### Phase 1 — Verify the portfolio

- Inventory active projects and assign each a public name.
- Record owner, role, status, audience, evidence, link, confidentiality level, and desired collaboration.
- Separate personal work, academic work, WHO work, and joint or institutional work.
- Validate every quantitative claim already on the site.
- Decide which projects are flagship, supporting evidence, private, paused, or archived.

**Output:** approved public project register.

### Phase 2 — Set the narrative

- Confirm the recommended positioning or revise it.
- Choose 3–5 flagship projects that together demonstrate the complete operating cycle.
- Agree the main homepage promise and collaboration invitation.
- Define language for responsible AI, institutional affiliation, and ownership.

**Output:** approved message hierarchy and page outlines.

### Phase 3 — Rewrite core pages

- Rewrite Home first.
- Rebuild Builds around portfolio categories and project maturity.
- Tighten About and add the forward-looking synthesis.
- Turn Contact into a specific Collaborate page.
- Reorganise Field Notes and prepare the first substantive notes.

**Output:** reviewed page copy in Markdown before HTML changes.

### Phase 4 — Implement and verify

- Update HTML while preserving the simple static architecture.
- Keep navigation identical on every page.
- Add only the CSS components required by the new content.
- Bump the `style.css` query version across every HTML page if CSS changes.
- Check responsive layouts, dark mode, metadata, links, accessibility, and page consistency.
- Review the local site before deployment.

**Output:** deploy-ready static site.

### Phase 5 — Maintain the evidence

- Establish a lightweight project-status and field-note publishing routine.
- Review quantitative claims quarterly.
- Publish short learning notes from pilots and builds.
- Keep a visible “what I am testing now” section current.

**Output:** a site that reflects active work instead of becoming a static biography.
